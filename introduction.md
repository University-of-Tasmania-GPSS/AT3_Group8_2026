# Introduction
## Abstract
Coastal erosion does not always result in visible shoreline retreat. Where a seawall becomes 
damaged, water may enter behind the structure and gradually remove supporting material. This 
process can create subsurface voids, potentially causing the ground above to subside or collapse. Erosion was assessed as a key contributor to the sink hole seen at Sandy Bay meaning it was to be included in the model. There were however no accurate datasets available for the Derwent Estuary which modelled erosion. This is why in this project, an erosion model was first established which was then passed into the final sinkhole model. 


# 
## Study Area and Datasets
The investigation focused on the Derwent Estuary with the Long Beach shoreline and a 100 m landward coastal corridor where the original sinkhole was, a key focus point. 
Spatial analysis was undertaken using GDA2020 / MGA Zone 55 (EPSG:7855), with the primary 
susceptibility models generated at a 5 m raster resolution. 

The datasets included:
•	1 metre ELVIS digital elevation model (DEM) (ICSM, n.d.)

•	Georeferenced Coastline (Tasmanian Government, n.d.)

•   Reconnaissance Soil Types (Tasmanian Government, n.d.)

•   MRT Geological Classification  (Mineral Resources Tasmania, 2021)

•	2010 and 2100 Storm Surge Data (Tasmanian Government, n.d.)

•   ERA5 Wind Data (ECMWF, 2026)

•   LIST Coastal Vulnerability (Tasmanian Government, n.d.)
 
The selected variables represented the environmental conditions potentially influencing coastal 
erosion and washout. However, mapped natural soils and geological units do not necessarily describe the 
engineering properties of material placed behind a seawall meaning this model does not neccessarily reflect the local changes in sinkhole susceptibility due to sea wall erosion.
